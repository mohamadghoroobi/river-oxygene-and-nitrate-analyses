Seasonal and spatial patterns<br/>in river water quality

Oxygen and nitrate concentrations across five river sections

Mohamad Ghoroobi

17 November 2026

Application Report<br/>M.Sc. Data Science<br/>TU Dortmund University<br/>Summer Semester 2027

Abstract

This report examines 900 measurements of oxygen and nitrate in a balanced design of five river sections and four seasons. A factorial cell-means model with heteroskedasticity-robust inference compares spring with autumn and evaluates section differences. After Holm adjustment across 26 tests, nitrate differs between spring and autumn and between every section pair. Oxygen shows section differences and a section-by-season interaction, but no statistically significant averaged spring-autumn difference. All inference is conditional on independent sampling, which cannot be verified from the supplied data.

Research questions

First, do spring and autumn differ in their mean oxygen and nitrate concentrations? Second, do mean concentrations differ between river sections, and which pairs differ? Both questions concern concentrations measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L).

Reading the results

Seasonal contrasts average equally over the five sections. Section contrasts average equally over the four seasons. The model retains interactions, so these averages do not imply a common effect in every season or section. Two-sided tests use a familywise significance level of 0.05. All reported adjusted p-values belong to one family of 26 tests.

The report includes two statistical figures and seven tables. The repository also supplies cell-level descriptive statistics, all pointwise confidence intervals, model diagnostics, provenance records, and executable analysis and report-generation code. These materials support reproduction without extending the ten-page report.

1. Introduction and data

A comparison of water-quality measurements should distinguish spatial patterns from seasonal variation. Pooling all observations can obscure differences that change across the year. Here, oxygen and nitrate are analyzed separately while preserving the same section-season structure. The aim is to quantify observed differences and their statistical uncertainty, without attributing them to an unmeasured physical or biological cause.

TU Dortmund University provides the application task and the data file <i>waterquality.csv</i> (TU Dortmund University, 2026a, 2026b). The dataset contains 900 rows. Each of five ordered sections has 180 observations: 45 in spring, summer, autumn, and winter. The section labels run from headwaters (A1) through upper course (A2), middle course (A3), and lower course (A4) to the mouth (A5).

Table 1 describes the variables. Oxygen and nitrate are quantitative concentrations in mg/L. Season is treated as a categorical factor rather than a numeric trend. River section is ordered geographically, but is modeled categorically because no distances are supplied. ID is excluded from the statistical model.

Variable | Meaning | Treatment

ID | Row identifier | Validation only

river_section | Five river sections | Categorical factor

saison | Four seasons | Categorical factor

oxygen_mgL | Oxygen concentration | Numeric outcome, mg/L

nitrate_mgL | Nitrate concentration | Numeric outcome, mg/L

Table 1. Variables in the original semicolon-separated CSV.

Validation found no missing values, duplicate IDs, duplicate complete rows, negative concentrations, or nonfinite values. All 20 section-season cells contain exactly 45 observations. No measurements were removed or imputed. These checks establish consistency with the task description; they do not verify the measurement process.

The file contains no sampling dates, repeated-site identifiers, collection protocol, or confirmed river name. The analysis therefore assumes independent observations conditionally within the design. This assumption is a limitation, not a conclusion from the IDs. The principal findings are a spring-autumn nitrate difference, a downstream nitrate gradient, and a season-dependent oxygen pattern. Sections 2-3 define the methods, Sections 4-6 present results, and Section 7 discusses their limits.

2. Statistical model and estimands

2.1 A factorial model without additive restrictions

For each outcome separately, let Y<sub>ijk</sub> be observation k in section i and season j, with i = 1,...,5, j = 1,...,4, and k = 1,...,45. The full factorial model is written in cell-means form:

$Y_{ijk}=\mu_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ijk},\qquad E(\varepsilon_{ijk})=0,\quad \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{ijk})=\sigma_{ij}^{2}.$

This is equivalent to an intercept, section effects, season effects, and their interaction under suitable identifying constraints (Faraway, 2014, Chapters 14-16). In matrix form Y = X beta + epsilon, X has one indicator column for each of the 20 cells. The least-squares estimate is beta-hat = (X′X)<super>-1</super>X′Y, which equals the vector of sample cell means. There are 900 - 20 = 880 residual degrees of freedom.

2.2 Contrasts that answer the questions

The seasonal estimand is the equally weighted spring-autumn difference. The section estimand is the difference between two section means, each averaged over all seasons:

$\Delta=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}(\mu_{i,\mathrm{spring}}-\mu_{i,\mathrm{autumn}}),\qquad D_{ab}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4}(\mu_{aj}-\mu_{bj}).$

Because the dataset is balanced, these equal-weight averages also equal the corresponding pooled sample means. Explicit contrasts retain the intended weights if the implementation is reviewed. A positive Delta means a higher spring concentration; a positive D<sub>ab</sub> means a higher concentration in section a. Effect estimates remain in the original mg/L units.

2.3 Covariance estimation

Errors may have different variances across cells. HC3 covariance uses residuals e<sub>r</sub> and diagonal leverages h<sub>r</sub> of X(X′X)<super>-1</super>X′ (Davidson and MacKinnon, 2004, Section 5.5):

$\widehat V_{\mathrm{HC3}}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{e_r^2}{(1-h_r)^2}\right)X(X^\top X)^{-1}.$

Here h<sub>r</sub> = 1/45, and the HC3 variance of cell mean ij simplifies to s<sub>ij</sub><super>2</super>/44, where s<sub>ij</sub><super>2</super> is the usual sample variance. The covariance matrix is diagonal under independent sampling. A direct cell-based calculation reproduces the software covariance to numerical precision. HC3 addresses heteroskedasticity; it does not correct dependence between observations.

3. Testing, multiplicity and diagnostics

3.1 Null hypotheses and reference distributions

For each analyte, the seasonal null is H<sub>0</sub>: Delta = 0 against Delta unequal to 0. The omnibus section null states that all five equally weighted section means are equal; four linearly independent contrasts test it. Each of ten section-pair nulls states D<sub>ab</sub> = 0. Finally, 12 independent difference-in-differences contrasts test the absence of interaction: (mu<sub>ij</sub> - mu<sub>i1</sub>) - (mu<sub>1j</sub> - mu<sub>11</sub>) = 0 for i &gt; 1 and j &gt; 1.

$W=(L\hat\beta)^\top(L\widehat V_{\mathrm{HC3}}L^\top)^{-1}(L\hat\beta),\qquad W\ \dot\sim\ \chi_q^2.$

L contains the q independent contrast rows. For q = 1, z = (c′ beta-hat)/sqrt(c′ V-hat c) is compared with a standard normal distribution; W = z<super>2</super>. These are large-sample approximations rather than exact finite-sample tests. Pointwise 95% confidence intervals are estimate plus or minus 1.96 times the HC3 standard error. Robust Wald inference is described by Davidson and MacKinnon (2004); the implementation uses statsmodels (2026).

3.2 A single multiplicity family

The family comprises 26 tests: two seasonal, two omnibus section, twenty pairwise section, and two interaction tests. Holm adjustment controls the probability of at least one false rejection at alpha = 0.05 when the individual p-values are valid. For ordered p-values p<sub>(1)</sub> through p<sub>(26)</sub>, define:

$p^{\mathrm{adj}}_{(k)}=\min\!\left\{1,\ \max_{1\leq r\leq k}(26-r+1)p_{(r)}\right\}. $

Reject when the adjusted p-value is at most 0.05. The procedure permits arbitrary dependence between tests (Holm, 1979; Hochberg and Tamhane, 1987). Including both analytes in one family avoids treating their related conclusions as separate opportunities for rejection. The 95% intervals remain pointwise and should not be read as simultaneous intervals corresponding to the adjusted decisions.

3.3 Descriptive methods and diagnostic checks

Tables report means, sample SDs with denominator n - 1, medians, quartiles, and ranges. A boxplot spans quartiles Q1 to Q3, marks the median, and extends whiskers to the furthest observations within 1.5(Q3 - Q1) of the box. Remaining points are displayed and retained. Quartiles use linear interpolation. Residual-versus-fitted and normal Q-Q plots inspect spread and shape; diagnostics follow the approach in Faraway (2014, Chapter 6). HC2 covariance provides a sensitivity check. No outcome-based observation deletion or model selection is used.

7. Discussion and conclusions

7.1 Diagnostics and robustness

Table 7 summarizes within-cell variability and residual shape. Cell SDs vary, supporting the use of a covariance estimator that allows unequal variances. Residual skewness is close to zero for both outcomes. The residual Q-Q and fitted-value plots are supplied as Figure 3 in the repository; they show no pronounced overall curvature in the Q-Q relationship. This visual check cannot prove normality or independence.

Diagnostic | Oxygen | Nitrate

Within-cell SD range (mg/L) | 0.648-1.090 | 1.181-1.746

Largest/smallest cell variance | 2.828 | 2.187

Residual skewness | -0.009 | -0.008

Maximum |standardized residual| | 3.472 | 3.634

Table 7. Model diagnostics. Standardized residuals use the model residual mean square and leverage; all measurements remain in the analysis.

Replacing HC3 by HC2 scales each cell-mean variance by 44/45 and leaves all 26 adjusted decisions unchanged. A pooled-variance seasonal calculation also leaves oxygen nonsignificant and nitrate significant. These checks address covariance choice, not errors in the sampling protocol. Tests remain asymptotic, and HC3 alone cannot correct spatial or temporal correlation.

7.2 Answers and interpretation

For spring versus autumn, the analysis finds higher mean nitrate in spring, averaged across sections, but insufficient evidence of an oxygen difference after correction. For river sections, both analytes show differences in their marginal means. Every nitrate pair differs, with higher means progressively downstream. The oxygen pairs identified in Table 6 differ on average over seasons, while its significant interaction indicates that spatial differences vary through the year. Failure to reject any other pair does not show that its means are equal.

The nitrate gradient and the low summer oxygen values identify patterns worth investigating, but the dataset contains no covariates that explain them. There is no basis here for attributing changes to agriculture, discharge, temperature, or another mechanism. Statistical significance does not establish environmental harm or regulatory noncompliance; that would require applicable standards and additional contextual measurements.

7.3 Limits and reproducibility

The main limitation is the unknown sampling design. Repeated samples from the same site or sampling occasion could invalidate the assumed covariance structure. Future analysis should obtain dates, site identifiers, hydrological information, and measurement protocols, then use a suitable dependence model. The present conclusions apply conditionally to the supplied design, without a claim of representativeness for other rivers or years.
