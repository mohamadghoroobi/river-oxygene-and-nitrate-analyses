1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation and research questions
Spatial and seasonal comparisons provide two complementary views of river water quality. A con-
centration measured near the headwaters may differ from one measured near the mouth; a seasonal
average may also conceal a spatial pattern that changes through the year. Both sources of variation
should therefore be considered when comparing measurements. This report examines oxygen and
nitrate in the Saubach river, using the river name stipulated in the task.
The analysis addresses two questions. First, do spring and autumn differ in mean oxygen or nitrate
concentration? Second, do mean concentrations differ among the five river sections, and which
section pairs differ? All concentrations and estimated differences are expressed in milligrams per
liter(mg/L).Theseasonalcomparisonaveragesequallyoversections,whileeachspatialcomparison
averages equally over seasons.
1.2 Approach and principal findings
Thesupplieddatasetcontains900observationsinabalanceddesign: fiveriversections,fourseasons,
and 45 observations in each section–season combination. Descriptive summaries and figures show
the location, spread, and shape of the concentrations. A full factorial model retains the section-by-
season interaction. Heteroskedasticity-robust contrasts then address the research questions. Holm
adjustmentisappliedacrossbothanalytestolimittheprobabilityofatleastonefalserejection,using
the specified significance level 𝛼 = 0.075.
The main findings are as follows. Mean nitrate is higher in spring than autumn by 0.871 mg/L, and
every section pair differs for nitrate after adjustment. Mean oxygen is higher in spring by 0.152
mg/L, but that comparison does not reject after adjustment. Seven oxygen section pairs differ when
averaged over seasons. Oxygen also shows a section-by-season interaction, so its spatial differences
should not be described as constant throughout the year.
These conclusions depend on the assumed independence of the observations. The data do not pro-
vide sampling dates or repeated-site identifiers with which to verify this assumption. The report
quantifies the observed patterns without attributing them to an unmeasured environmental cause.
Section 2 describes the data and estimands. Section 3 defines and justifies the methods. Section 4
presents the descriptive and inferential results. Section 5 answers the questions and discusses limi-
tations.

2.2 Validation and information limits
All 900 observations were retained. Validation found no missing values, duplicate IDs, duplicate
complete rows, negative concentrations, or nonfinite values. The expected 45 observations were
present in every section–season cell. No imputation or outcome-based exclusion was performed.
Sectionismodeledcategoricallybecausenodistancesbetweensectionsaresupplied. Seasonisalso
categorical. The ID is neither a predictor nor evidence that measurements are temporally ordered
or paired. Oxygen and nitrate appear in the same rows, but the two analytes are modeled separately;
multiplicity correction includes both outcomes.
The file does not give sampling dates, repeated-site identifiers, collection methods, or measurement
protocols. Independent errors are therefore an explicit assumption. Without additional metadata,
neither representativeness for other rivers or years nor a causal explanation of the observed differ-

5 Summary and discussion
5.1 Answers to the research questions
The analysis compared oxygen and nitrate concentrations in the Saubach river across five sections
andfourseasons. All900observationswereretainedinabalanceddesign. Testsusedafullfactorial
model, HC3 covariance, and Holm adjustment across 26 hypotheses at 𝛼 = 0.075.
For spring versus autumn, mean nitrate is higher in spring by 0.871 mg/L, averaged equally over the
five sections (adjusted 𝑝 = 1.22 × 10−8). Mean oxygen is higher in spring by 0.152 mg/L, but its
adjusted 𝑝 = 0.324 does not provide sufficient evidence of a difference. This non-rejection does not
demonstrate equal concentrations.
Both analytes show differences between section means averaged over seasons. Every nitrate pair
differs, with progressively higher means downstream. For oxygen, A1 differs from all four other
sections, and A4 also differs from A2, A3, and A5. The oxygen interaction shows that its spatial
pattern depends on season, so these seven pairwise differences are averages rather than a common
pattern throughout the year.
5.2 Diagnostic evidence and limitations
Within-cell SDs range from 0.648 to 1.090 mg/L for oxygen and 1.181 to 1.746 mg/L for nitrate,
supporting a covariance estimator that permits different variances. Residual Q–Q plots show ap-
proximately linear overall patterns, with some tail departures. The residual plots and full diagnos-
tics are supplied with the analysis code. Replacing HC3 with HC2 leaves all 26 adjusted decisions
unchanged. Direct cell calculations also reproduce the estimated means and HC3 covariance to
numerical precision.
The main limitation is the unknown sampling design. The CSV does not identify sampling dates
or repeated sites. If measurements are correlated in time or space, the independent-error covari-
ance may understate or otherwise misrepresent uncertainty; HC3 does not solve that problem. The
significance conclusions are therefore conditional on independent sampling and the accuracy of
asymptotic approximation.
The downstream nitrate gradient and low summer oxygen identify patterns for further investigation.
The data do not establish their causes or whether concentrations imply environmental harm. Ad-
ditional sampling metadata and contextual measurements would be needed before considering a
dependence model, explanatory mechanisms, or applicable environmental thresholds.
